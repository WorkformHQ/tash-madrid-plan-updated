Madrid 101 — The Practical Guide

Everything a barber setting up independently actually needs to know before landing in Spain — legal, financial, and on-the-ground

📋
Autónomo — Self-Employment Registration
In Spain, self-employed workers register as autónomo. This is your equivalent of being incorporated as a sole proprietor in Canada. You'll need this to legally take clients and issue invoices.

How to register: Through the Agencia Tributaria (Spanish tax authority) online or at a local office. You'll need your NIE (foreigner's ID number) first — get this before you leave Canada if possible, through the Spanish consulate.

Cost: As of 2026, the minimum autónomo social security contribution is approximately €230–€290/month (sliding scale based on income). This is mandatory regardless of earnings in your first year.

Flat-rate deal: New autónomos get a reduced rate of €80/month for the first 12 months. This is significant — budget around it.
🪪
NIE — Your Most Important Document
The Número de Identificación de Extranjero (NIE) is your Spanish ID for foreigners. You cannot open a bank account, sign a lease, or register as autónomo without it.

Get it before you leave Canada: Apply at the Spanish consulate in Toronto. Bring passport, two passport photos, and the completed EX-15 form. Appointments are limited — book as early as possible, ideally 6–9 months before your move.

In Spain: If you didn't get it before arrival, apply at a Comisaría de Policía (police station) or Oficina de Extranjería. Expect waits of 1–3 months for an appointment in Madrid.

Bottom line: Treat NIE like a passport. Start the process now — even two years out.
💼
Canadian Visa Route for Spain
As a Canadian citizen, you can stay in Spain visa-free for 90 days. For longer stays to work, you need a visa.

Best option for you — Non-Lucrative Visa + Autónomo: Arrive on a non-lucrative visa (shows you have savings), then convert to autónomo status after establishing residency. Many expat barbers use this path.

Digital Nomad Visa: Spain launched a Digital Nomad Visa in 2023. As a self-employed barber, you may qualify if you can demonstrate you serve clients remotely (content creation, online consulting) alongside in-person services.

Get an immigration lawyer: Budget €500–€1,000 for a consultation with a Spanish immigration attorney. Worth every euro. Many work remotely with Canadian clients preparing to move.
💶
Spanish Tax as a Barber
As an autónomo in Spain, you'll file quarterly tax returns (IVA — VAT — and IRPF — income tax).

IVA (VAT): Hairdressing and barbering services are taxed at 10% IVA (reduced rate, not the full 21%). You collect this from clients and remit quarterly.

IRPF (Income tax withholding): You apply a 15% withholding on your invoices to professional clients (like salons). For direct client bookings, you declare quarterly.

Practical tip: Use a gestora — a Spanish accountant/admin specialist — for about €60–€100/month. They handle all quarterly filings so you focus on barbering, not paperwork. Non-negotiable for a first-year foreign autónomo.
🏦
Banking in Spain
You'll need a Spanish bank account to receive payments, pay autónomo contributions, and sign leases.

Best options for new arrivals:
— BBVA: English-speaking support, good app, easy for foreigners to open.
— Sabadell: Known for being expat-friendly, English service available.
— N26 or Wise: Open now (before you leave Canada) for fee-free EUR transactions and as a bridge account. Wise is especially good for converting CAD → EUR without bank fees.

Open Wise now: Seriously. Start using it to convert savings into EUR. Exchange rates have shifted significantly in the past two years and locking in gradually de-risks your fund.
💱
Converting Your $50K to EUR
As of mid-2026, CAD/EUR is approximately 0.67–0.70. That means your $50K CAD ≈ €33,500–€35,000 EUR.

Don't convert all at once: Exchange rates move. Converting $5K–$8K CAD every couple of months (called "dollar-cost averaging" for currency) smooths out rate volatility.

Use Wise, not your bank: Banks charge 2–3% in hidden exchange fees. Wise charges ~0.4%. On $50K, that's the difference between paying ~$1,200 in fees vs. ~$200. The savings is real.

Set a rate alert: If CAD strengthens above 0.72 EUR, that's a good time to convert a larger chunk. Wise and Google Finance both have rate alerts.
💳
Accepting Payments as a Barber in Madrid
Cash is still common in Madrid barbershops but declining fast, especially in expat-heavy neighbourhoods.

Best payment setup:
— Sumup: Popular card reader in Spain, low fees (~1.69%), works with your Spanish bank account.
— Zettle (PayPal): Also widely used, good app.
— Bizum: Spain's instant bank transfer app (like Interac e-transfer). Once you have a Spanish bank account, virtually every Spanish client uses this. Set it up day one.

For bookings: Vagaro works in Spain — update currency to EUR, timezone to CET, and you're set. Keep using it. Don't switch booking systems in your first year.
🏥
Healthcare as an Autónomo
One of the biggest anxieties for Canadians moving from a public healthcare country: what happens if you get sick?

Good news: Once you're registered as autónomo and paying social security contributions, you have access to Spain's public healthcare system (Sistema Nacional de Salud) — which is genuinely excellent.

Coverage timeline: Social security contributions give you access to public health within a few months of registration. Dental is mostly private.

Bridge insurance: Get private travel/health insurance for your first 3–6 months in Spain before autónomo coverage kicks in. Budget €80–€150/month. Allianz Care and AXA are common choices for Canadian expats in Spain.

OHIP: You lose OHIP coverage after 212 days outside Ontario. Plan accordingly.

Chair Rental vs. Your Own Space — Year One
You currently run your own space in Toronto. In Madrid year one, the math changes.

Chair rental first: In Madrid, renting a chair (silla en alquiler) from an existing barbershop or salon typically costs €400–€800/month. You keep all your revenue above that. Zero fit-out cost, no long lease commitment.

Your own space later: Commercial leases in Madrid typically require a 2-month deposit + first month upfront. Fit-out costs for even a modest barbershop: €5,000–€15,000.

Strategy: Year 1 — rent a chair in Malasaña, build clientele. Year 2 — if revenue supports it, negotiate your own space or a revenue-share arrangement with the host salon.

Running your own space in Toronto is a skill that transfers. Use it as leverage, not a starting requirement.

Spanish Commercial Lease Basics
If you go straight to your own space, know what you're signing.

Minimum term: Spanish commercial leases (Ley de Arrendamientos Urbanos) typically have a minimum 5-year term for the tenant's protection — but you can negotiate shorter terms with the landlord's agreement. Aim for 1-year renewable.

Fianza (deposit): Typically 2 months' rent, held in escrow by the regional government (in Madrid, the IVIMA). This is legally required.

IBI and community fees: Check who pays property tax and community of owners fees — sometimes passed to tenants, sometimes not. Always ask.

Licencia de apertura: You'll need an opening licence (activity licence) from the Ayuntamiento de Madrid to legally operate a barbershop. Budget €300–€800 and 4–8 weeks. A gestoría can handle this for you.

What Life Actually Looks Like in Madrid
The practical texture of daily life — so the move feels real, not abstract.

Working hours: Madrid runs late. Lunch is 2–4pm, dinner starts at 9–10pm. Barbershop peak hours are typically 11am–2pm and 5–9pm. Plan your schedule accordingly.

Siesta culture: Less common than the stereotype, especially in central Madrid, but many small shops still close 2–5pm. As an autónomo, your hours are yours — this is an advantage.

Weather: Hot summers (35–40°C in July/August). Mild winters. Budget for aircon in your workspace — non-negotiable for clients in summer.

Social life: Madrileños are warm but slower to befriend than Canadians. Join expat groups early (Internations, Facebook groups) for a quick community while local friendships grow organically over 6–12 months.

What to Bring, What to Leave
Bring your tools: Your clippers, guards, scissors, and straight razors. Professional-grade barbering tools are available in Spain but your specific setup is personal. Wahl and Andis are available; some brands are harder to find.

Voltage: Spain uses 220V / 50Hz. Canada uses 120V / 60Hz. Most modern clippers (Wahl, Andis) are dual-voltage — check the label. If not, you need a transformer, not just an adapter.

Furniture / Barber chair: Don't ship. Buy in Spain. Shipping a barber chair from Canada costs more than buying a quality used one in Madrid. Check Wallapop (Spain's Kijiji) for secondhand professional equipment.

Leave: Most furniture, appliances, anything heavy. Storage in Toronto or sell it. The cost of shipping rarely makes financial sense vs. buying secondhand in Madrid.
